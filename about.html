About

Who runs Northline Yoga, and how the classes get made

Northline Yoga LLC is a small operation. One instructor, six classes, and a fairly stubborn opinion about what a short session should contain.

Northline Yoga is run by Ellen Marsh, a registered yoga teacher based in the Upper Midwest. Before teaching, Ellen coached masters swimmers for eleven years, which is where the approach came from. Swimmers turn up strong, motivated and completely locked up through the shoulders and hips. They did not want a spiritual practice. They wanted twenty minutes that would let them reach overhead without shrugging.

That is still the brief. Northline Yoga is built for adults roughly between thirty and fifty-five who already do something physical — walking, swimming, lifting, cycling, chasing kids around a yard — and who have noticed that the range of motion they had at twenty-five is quietly getting smaller. The classes are short, they name what they are working on, and they leave out anything that would need explaining twice.

The approach

Three ideas run through everything on this site.

Strength and mobility are the same project. Range of motion you cannot control is not much use. If your hamstrings loosen up but your hips have no strength at the new end of the range, you are more likely to feel it in your lower back, not less. So the classes pair the two: you open a position, then you hold it under load. A low lunge is a stretch for thirty seconds and a strength exercise for the next thirty.

Short and repeated beats long and occasional. A ninety-minute class once a fortnight is a nice afternoon. It does not change how a joint moves. Fifteen minutes, three times a week, does — and it survives contact with a real calendar. That is why nothing in the library runs past thirty minutes and why the starter plan asks for three sessions rather than seven.

Props are equipment, not a downgrade. A block under your hand in a standing forward fold does not make the pose easier in a way that matters. It brings the floor up to where your hands actually are, so your spine stays long and your hamstrings get the load instead of your lower back rounding to make up the difference. Nobody in these classes is working toward the day they stop using blocks.

How a class gets built

Each session is written the same way, in four parts.

It opens with two or three minutes of joint preparation — ankles, hips and shoulders moved through small circles and slow reaches before anything is asked to hold. Then a warm sequence of two or three linked shapes, repeated until they stop feeling stiff. Then the main block, which is the reason the class exists: the hip holds, the standing strength work, the shoulder rotations. And it closes with two minutes on the floor, breathing slowly, with no instruction to empty your mind or picture anything.

The main block is the only part that changes much between classes. Everything else is deliberately familiar so you can stop thinking about the format and pay attention to what your body is doing.

Timing is honest. A class listed at 18 minutes runs 18 minutes, not 18 plus a five-minute introduction. If a session needs a chair or a wall, that is stated before you press play, not two minutes in when you are already on the floor.

Safety and modifications

Every pose in the library is taught with at least one alternative. Standing balances have a chair version. Deep hip work has a version done lying down. Anything overhead has a strap option for people whose shoulders do not yet get there without the ribs flaring.

There are a few rules that do not change:

  • Sharp is a stop signal. A broad stretching feeling in the belly of a muscle is the work. Sharp, pinching or electrical sensations, especially near a joint, mean come out of the shape.
  • Nothing is held past the point where breathing changes. If you cannot breathe slowly in a position, you are past the useful part of it.
  • No weight on the head or neck. There are no headstands, shoulder stands or neck-loaded shapes anywhere in the library.
  • Both sides, every time. If a sequence works one leg, the other gets the same duration, even when it feels easier.

What we do not do

We do not run challenges, streak counters or leaderboards. We do not publish before-and-after photographs. We do not tell you what a session will do to your weight, your sleep or your resting heart rate, because we have no way of knowing that and neither does anybody else selling you a video. When we describe a benefit, it is either a description of what the movement asks of a muscle, or it is something members have reported to us — and we label it that way.

If that sounds dull, it probably is, in the way a well-made training handout is dull. That is the intent.
