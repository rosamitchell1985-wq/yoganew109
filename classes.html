Classes

Six classes, in detail

What is in each session, how long it runs, what you need within reach, and who tends to get the most out of it.

The library is deliberately small. Six sessions is enough variety to cover the joints that matter and few enough that you will actually get better at them. Most members settle into two favourites and rotate a third in when something feels stiff.

Foundations — 15 minutes

Eight shapes, taught one at a time, with the prop version shown before the full version. These are the positions the rest of the library assembles into sequences: low lunge, high lunge, forward fold, chair, warrior two, side angle, seated twist and a supported bridge. You hold each one long enough to hear where it should be felt and to make one correction.

Who it suits: anyone new to yoga, and anyone returning after a long break who wants the vocabulary before the pace. It is also the right pick if you have tried a drop-in class and spent it looking sideways at other people to work out what was happening.

Hips & Hamstrings — 20 minutes

The most-used class in the library. It works the back of the legs and the front of the hips, which are the two areas that stiffen fastest if you sit for a living or train in a straight line. Holds run five to eight slow breaths. A strap loops around the foot so you can lengthen a hamstring without hauling on it with your hands and rounding your back to compensate.

Who it suits: lifters whose squat depth is limited by their hips, runners and cyclists with permanently short hip flexors, and anyone whose hamstrings feel tight the moment they stand up from a chair.

Shoulders & Upper Back — 18 minutes

Built for desk workers. The session moves the shoulder blades before it moves the arms: slides, rotations and reaches done slowly, then chest opening across a block, then overhead work with a strap held wide. There is thoracic rotation on hands and knees, which is the movement most people have lost without noticing, and a short neck sequence at the end that stays well inside a comfortable range.

Who it suits: anyone who spends most of the day at a keyboard, swimmers with tight lats, and lifters who feel their shoulders shrug up toward their ears on an overhead press.

Standing Strength — 25 minutes

The hardest session in the library, and the one that most resembles a workout. Warrior one, warrior two, side angle, chair and a low squat are strung into a short loop, repeated on each side, with the holds getting longer as the class goes on. Legs stay under load for most of the twenty-five minutes. Blocks sit at the front of the mat so you can drop a hand down rather than tipping your torso to reach the floor.

Who it suits: people who already train and want the yoga session to count as work, not as a warm-down. Skip it or halve the holds on the day after heavy leg training.

Balance Work — 15 minutes

Balance is a skill, and like any skill it fades if you stop practising. This class works the feet and ankles first, then moves through single-leg holds with progressively less support: two fingertips on a chair, then one, then none if you want it. There is a short section done with the eyes closed, which is optional and clearly flagged. A chair or a clear stretch of wall stays within arm's reach for the whole session.

Who it suits: everyone over forty, honestly. Also anyone who has rolled an ankle in the last couple of years and has quietly stopped trusting that foot.

Long Stretch — 30 minutes

Everything on the floor, nothing rushed, holds of one to three minutes. Supported hip openers over a cushion, a long strap-assisted hamstring sequence, a gentle twist on each side and several minutes of quiet lying-down time at the end. There is no strength element and no standing. It is the class members put on the evening after a hard training day or at the end of a week that went badly.

Who it suits: anyone on a recovery day, anyone who trains hard five or six days a week, and anyone who finds that a short session leaves them wanting more floor time.

Equipment

The whole library runs on a mat, two blocks and a strap. Blocks should be firm — cork or dense foam, not the squashy kind. A strap can be a proper yoga strap, a dressing-gown belt or a bath towel. Balance Work wants a chair that will not slide. Long Stretch is more comfortable with a firm cushion or a folded blanket under the hips. Nothing needs shoes, and nothing needs a wall hook, a machine or added weight.

Choosing where to start

If you are new to yoga, start with Foundations and repeat it three times before you move on. If you already stretch after training and know the basic shapes, start with Hips & Hamstrings, because that is where most active adults are actually restricted. If your complaint is upper-body — a stiff neck, shoulders that will not go overhead cleanly — start with Shoulders & Upper Back and add Long Stretch on the weekend.

Whichever you pick, the pattern that works is three sessions a week with a day off. The practice guide goes through how to schedule that around whatever else you are training, and when to take an extra rest day instead of pushing through.

Every class has modifications, but none of them is a substitute for professional advice about your own body. If you have an existing injury or a diagnosed joint or spinal condition, talk to your physician or physical therapist before starting. Stop immediately if a movement produces sharp or pinching pain.
