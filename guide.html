Practice guide

How often should you practise, and when to rest

A working answer for adults who already train: how many sessions a week, where to put them, and the signals that mean take the day off instead.

The short answer

Three sessions a week, fifteen to thirty minutes each, with at least one full rest day. That is the pattern that most Northline members settle into and stay with. It is enough exposure for a joint to notice, and it is small enough that a busy fortnight does not wipe it out.

Two sessions a week maintains what you have. Three moves you forward. Four or five is fine if the extra ones are short and easy, and unhelpful if they are all Standing Strength.

Why three

Mobility is partly tissue and mostly nervous system. When a hamstring feels short, a large part of what you are feeling is your body limiting the range because it does not trust you there. Trust is built by repetition, not by intensity, and repetition means frequency. One long session a week gives your nervous system very little to go on. Three short ones give it a pattern.

The strength half works the other way round — muscle responds to load and needs recovery time between exposures. Three sessions with gaps between them fits both requirements without either one interfering with the other.

Fitting it around what you already do

If you lift

Put Hips & Hamstrings or Shoulders & Upper Back on your lifting days, after the session or later in the evening. Do not do long static holds immediately before heavy lifting — save that for after. Standing Strength belongs on a non-lifting day, or on a day you trained upper body only. Long Stretch fits best on the day after your heaviest session.

If you run or cycle

Your hip flexors and calves take most of the load, and both stay short. Hips & Hamstrings twice a week is the highest-value choice you can make. Keep it away from the two hours before a hard run; the legs feel loose but not stable straight afterwards. Balance Work is worth adding if you run on trails or uneven ground.

If you swim

Shoulders & Upper Back, twice a week, every week. Swimming builds enormous strength through the lats and chest and slowly closes down thoracic rotation. Long Stretch on a non-swim evening covers the hips, which get almost nothing in the pool.

If yoga is the only thing you do

Then go four or five days a week and vary it: two Standing Strength, one Balance Work, one Hips & Hamstrings, one Long Stretch. Still take one full day completely off.

What a rest day is for

A rest day is not a day you failed to practise. Connective tissue adapts slowly and off the clock; strength gains land during recovery, not during the session. A rest day is when the work you already did becomes useful.

Take at least one a week where you do nothing structured. Walking, gardening and playing with your kids do not count against it. A twenty-minute mobility session does, so if you find yourself doing Long Stretch on your rest day every week, move the rest day.

Signals to stop or take an extra day

  • Sharp or pinching pain in or near a joint, in any position. Come out of the shape. Do not stretch into it to see whether it eases.
  • Soreness that has lasted more than three days from a previous session. That is a load you did not recover from.
  • Range that has gone backwards two sessions in a row. Usually a sign of accumulated fatigue rather than a mobility problem.
  • Poor sleep plus a raised resting heart rate for several days. Both belong to fatigue, and neither responds well to another session.
  • Any pain that radiates down an arm or a leg, or any numbness or tingling. Stop and speak to a clinician.
  • Genuine dread at the thought of the mat. Take two days off. If the feeling is still there, do Long Stretch instead of anything strenuous.

The signals above are general guidance for healthy adults, not a diagnostic checklist. Northline Yoga cannot assess an injury and does not try to. If pain is severe, persistent, or came on suddenly, contact your physician.

What progress actually looks like

Not a photograph. Progress here is quiet and shows up in ordinary places: getting your hand flat on the floor without bending your knees quite so much, holding a low lunge for eight breaths instead of four, standing on one leg to pull a sock on, reaching a shelf without hitching your ribs. Members typically report noticing something in the third or fourth week of consistent practice. Some notice sooner and some later, and neither means anything about you.

What we will not tell you is how much your range will change or how quickly. Nobody selling you a video knows that, and the number would depend on your history, your other training, your sleep and your age.

Getting the frequency to stick

Two things move the needle more than motivation. First, leave the mat out. The friction of unrolling a mat is small but it is enough to lose a fifteen-minute session on a tired evening. Second, attach the session to something that already happens — straight after you get home, straight after the kids are down, immediately before you shower. A session with a fixed slot survives a bad week; a session that happens "when I get a chance" does not.
